Here are 10 tokenization algorithms to build from scratch, ordered from beginner baselines to the advanced algorithms that power today's Large Language Models (LLMs).
Implementing this exact progression in your project tells a story: it shows hiring managers that you understand the historical evolution of NLP and exactly *why* modern subword algorithms were invented to solve the failures of earlier methods.
## Beginner: The Baselines
These are your starting points. They are fast to implement and set the baseline metrics (like compression ratio and vocabulary size) that your advanced algorithms will try to beat.
1. **Character-Level Tokenizer:** The simplest approach. It breaks text into individual letters and symbols (`"cat"` → `["c", "a", "t"]`).
* **Why build it:** It guarantees zero Out-Of-Vocabulary (OOV) errors, but you will quickly see its fatal flaw: it creates massive sequence lengths that overwhelm transformer context windows.
2. **Whitespace Tokenizer:** A naive word-level tokenizer that simply calls `.split(' ')`.
* **Why build it:** It shows the opposite extreme of character tokenization. It creates meaningful tokens but results in an infinitely large vocabulary size.
3. **Regex / Punctuation-Aware Tokenizer:** An upgrade to the whitespace approach. It uses regular expressions to isolate punctuation so that `"hello!"` becomes `["hello", "!"]` instead of a single, highly specific token.
## Intermediate: Structural & Statistical
These algorithms introduce logic to handle edge cases and capture more context than single words or characters.
4. **N-Gram Tokenizer:** Instead of single units, it groups characters or words into sliding windows of size *N* (e.g., bigrams or trigrams).
* **Why build it:** It teaches you how models historically captured local context (like `"New York"`) before self-attention mechanisms existed.

5. **Dictionary-Based (Maximum Matching):** An algorithm that relies on a predefined dictionary and greedily matches the longest possible sequence of characters.
* **Why build it:** This is crucial for understanding how to tokenize languages that don't use spaces, such as Chinese or Japanese.
6. **Naive Byte-Level Tokenizer:** Instead of mapping to Unicode characters, it converts raw text directly into 256 raw byte values.
* **Why build it:** It forces you to handle text at the absolute lowest level of computer memory, ensuring you never encounter an unknown character or broken emoji.
## Advanced: Modern Subword Algorithms
These are the industry standards. They aim for the "Goldilocks zone": balancing a compact vocabulary with short sequence lengths.
7. **Standard Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE):** Originally a data compression algorithm. It starts with a base vocabulary of characters and iteratively merges the most *frequently occurring* adjacent pairs in your training data until a target vocabulary size is reached.
8. **Byte-Level BPE (BBPE):** The algorithm that powered GPT-2 and continues to power modern OpenAI models. It combines the 256-byte base vocabulary of Algorithm #6 with the merging logic of Algorithm #7, resulting in a robust tokenizer that can handle any language natively.
9. **WordPiece:** Developed by Google for BERT. It looks similar to BPE, but instead of merging pairs based on raw frequency, it merges the pair that maximizes the *probability* (likelihood) of the training data.
10. **Unigram Language Model:** Used by models like T5 and Llama/Gemma (often wrapped in the **SentencePiece** library). Instead of starting small and merging up, Unigram starts with a massive vocabulary and iteratively *removes* tokens that contribute the least to the overall loss. SentencePiece also treats spaces as just another character (often represented as `_`), making it entirely language-agnostic.
